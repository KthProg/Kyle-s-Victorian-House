The History of 1147 McKinstry Street

(Formerly 405 McKinstry Avenue)

Origins and Pre-Development (Pre-1890s)

The land encompassing the lot originates from Private Claim 30, a 267-acre riverfront tract historically known as Spring Hill Farm. Originally claimed by Matthew Ernest—Detroit's first U.S. Collector of Customs appointed in 1800—the property was transferred by federal patent in 1823 to John R. Williams (Detroit's first post-1805 mayor) to settle Ernest's defaulted accounts.

The Williams family eventually subdivided the parent parcel, creating the J.C.D. Williams Subdivision (recorded in Plats, Liber 4, Page 20). This carved out Lot 42 (measuring 30 ft × 140 ft on the west side of McKinstry), which the Williams heirs sold to Theodore G. Williams, who in turn sold it on September 28, 1893, to Martin Steadly.

The 1890s: Construction and the Streetcar Suburb Boom

This era coincided with Detroit's explosive expansion as a streetcar suburb boomtown. Springwells Township, where the lot sits, was annexed by the city of Detroit in 1885, turning former farm tracts into dense grids of 30-foot working-class residential lots.

Martin Steadly secured a $500 mortgage on June 13, 1894, likely to initiate construction, followed by a $150 mortgage on April 21, 1896, presumably to complete the house. By the 1897 Sanborn fire map, the structure was fully standing, though the “copycat” house immediately to the north (later 1153 McKinstry) had not yet been built. Rather than living there, the Steadlys used the house as a rental property while residing on Dix Avenue (Vernor Highway).

The 1900s: Early Working-Class Tenants

Southwest Detroit at the turn of the century was defined by a steady influx of European immigrants—Irish, Germans, Scots, and Canadians—finding employment in the region's burgeoning mills, rail yards, and foundries.

The 1900 federal census lists Henry J. Stewart (a Scottish immigrant clerk who arrived in 1876) and his wife Daidee renting the house. Next door at what was then 403 McKinstry (a brick house), Irish immigrant and brasscaster John Kelly owned his home. By the end of the decade, the copycat house at 409 McKinstry was constructed, breaking up the open space.

The 1910s: Industrial Labor and Rental Stability

As Detroit rapidly transformed into the automotive capital of the world, neighborhoods near the industrial riverfront absorbed waves of laborers supporting the railroads and early auto factories.

Martin Steadly passed away, leaving his heirs (widow Anna and sons Frank and John) to manage the property from their residence on Ferdinand Avenue. The house remained a rental, occupied by William P. Breen, an Irish railroad switchman. Meanwhile, next door at the newly completed copycat house (409 McKinstry), Scottish locomotive engineer Daniel Black rented his home, underscoring the heavy rail and transit presence in the neighborhood.

The 1920s: Transition from Rentals to Family Ownership

The 1920s marked a monumental turning point for Southwest Detroit. While the auto boom drove intense industrial employment, this decade also saw the initial arrivals of Mexican immigrants settling around Bagley and West Vernor—laying the groundwork for Mexicantown. Meanwhile, municipal address modernization swept through Detroit, renumbering 405 McKinstry Avenue to 1147 McKinstry Street around the close of the decade.

William P. Breen remained a tenant through the early part of the decade, while the copycat house next door transitioned to owner-occupancy under William Johnson. On September 1, 1927, the Steadly heirs officially sold the property to John and Gertrude Puhek, ending its decades-long stint as a rental and integrating it into a multi-decade family legacy.

The 1930s–1950s: The Puhek Family Era and Mid-Century Detroit

This span encompassed the profound economic shock of the Great Depression, the mobilization of Detroit as the “Arsenal of Democracy” during World War II, and the initial post-war suburban migration waves. Unlike many areas that hollowed out quickly, Southwest Detroit retained strong immigrant and working-class community networks.

In the 1930 census, John Puhek is recorded as the owner-occupant of 1147 McKinstry. Following John's death by 1938, ownership navigated probate and consolidated within the family, transferring through daughter Mary Puhek (formalized in 1941 and 1943). The family's children—including Matthew J. Puhek (who graduated from the Henry Ford Trade School and became a tool and mechanical engineer with Snyder Corp.)—grew up amid this mid-century industrial peak. The house remained anchored by the Puhek family through the post-war decades, ensuring steady continuity while the surrounding city evolved.

The 1960s–1980s: Continuity Through Urban Shifts

During these decades, Detroit experienced significant demographic shifts, economic restructuring, and the acceleration of regional white flight. However, Southwest Detroit—bolstered by its strong Hispanic roots and the growing cultural identity of Mexicantown—retained its residential stability relative to other parts of the city.

In August 1960, ownership formally transitioned to Agnes Puhek, who maintained the property as part of the family's nearly 70-year tenure on McKinstry Street. The home quietly weathered the city's broader structural transitions while serving as a stable anchor on a working-class residential block.

The 1990s–Present: Cultural Shift and Modern Restoration

Mexicantown emerged as one of Detroit's most resilient and culturally vibrant neighborhoods, successfully bucking trends of abandonment through active community preservation, local commerce, and new waves of investment.

After nearly seven decades under the Puhek family, Agnes Puhek sold the house in 1996 to Mary J. Medina, reflecting the neighborhood's deep Mexican-American growth. In 1999, ownership transferred to Alfredo and Maria D. Garcia, who held the property for a quarter-century (utilizing it as a rental toward the latter half of their tenure). In June 2024, the house was purchased by Kyle Hooks, with the mortgage fully paid off that same month—marking a new chapter for the late-19th-century home as Mexicantown and Springwells experience renewed stability and appreciation in 21st-century Detroit.
