The History of 1147 McKinstry Street

(Formerly 405 McKinstry Avenue)

Origins and Pre-Development (Pre-1890s)

The land encompassing the lot originates from Private Claim 30, a 267-acre riverfront tract historically known as Spring Hill Farm. Originally claimed by Matthew Ernest—Detroit's first U.S. Collector of Customs, appointed by President John Adams in 1800—Ernest took possession before 1796 and the U.S. Land Board initially confirmed his title. When his customhouse accounts defaulted around the War of 1812 era, the government seized the land. In 1823, a federal patent transferred it to John R. Williams (Detroit's first post-1805 mayor) to satisfy Ernest's outstanding debts, cancelling the original patent in the process.

The Williams family eventually subdivided the parent parcel, creating the J.C.D. Williams Subdivision (recorded in Plats, Liber 4, Page 20). This carved out Lot 42 (measuring 30 ft × 140 ft on the west side of McKinstry). In January 1893, Flora Burt (sole legatee) and J.C. Devereaux Williams conveyed the lot to Theodore G. Williams, who in turn sold it on September 28, 1893, to Martin Steadley (Liber 421, Page 551)—the man who would build the house standing today.

The 1890s: Construction and the Streetcar Suburb Boom

This era coincided with Detroit's explosive expansion as a streetcar suburb boomtown. Springwells Township, where the lot sits, was annexed by the city of Detroit in 1885, turning former farm tracts into dense grids of 30-foot working-class residential lots. By 1892, the streets on McKinstry had been graded, sidewalks laid, and city water lines installed through the block.[3]

Martin Steadley secured a $500 mortgage on June 13, 1894, likely to initiate construction, followed by a $150 mortgage on April 21, 1896, presumably to complete the house. Rather than immediately renting it out, the Steadleys appear to have occupied the house themselves—a surprise party was held at 405 McKinstry in 1895,[4] indicating the family resided there at completion. By the 1897 Sanborn fire map, the structure was fully standing,[5] though the “copycat” house immediately to the north (later 1153 McKinstry) had not yet been built. The Steadleys eventually relocated to Dix Avenue (Vernor Highway) and transitioned the house to a rental property.

The 1900s: Early Working-Class Tenants

Southwest Detroit at the turn of the century was defined by a steady influx of European immigrants—Irish, Germans, Scots, and Canadians—finding employment in the region's burgeoning mills, rail yards, and foundries.

The 1900 federal census lists Henry J. Stewart (born March 1853 in Scotland, immigrated 1876) and his wife Daidee renting the house, where Stewart worked as a clerk.[6] Next door at 403 McKinstry (a brick house), Irish immigrant and brasscaster John Kelly owned his home. By 1904, the copycat house at 409 McKinstry had been completed—a neighbor's funeral held there that year confirms its existence.[7] Stewart had moved on by 1907, when W. T. Perring is recorded as the occupant of 405 McKinstry.[8]

The 1910s: Industrial Labor and Rental Stability

As Detroit rapidly transformed into the automotive capital of the world, neighborhoods near the industrial riverfront absorbed waves of laborers supporting the railroads and early auto factories.

Martin Steadley died on May 27, 1910, leaving his heirs—widow Anna and sons Frank and John—to manage the property from their residence at 659 Ferdinand Avenue. The house remained a rental, occupied by William P. Breen, an Irish railroad switchman. Breen and his wife welcomed a daughter in 1912[9] and a son in 1916 while living at 405 McKinstry. Meanwhile, next door at the copycat house (409 McKinstry), Scottish locomotive engineer Daniel Black rented his home, underscoring the heavy rail and transit presence in the neighborhood.

The 1920s: Transition from Rentals to Family Ownership

The 1920s marked a monumental turning point for Southwest Detroit. While the auto boom drove intense industrial employment, this decade also saw the initial arrivals of Mexican immigrants settling around Bagley and West Vernor—laying the groundwork for Mexicantown. Municipal address modernization swept through Detroit, renumbering 405 McKinstry Avenue to 1147 McKinstry Street around the close of the decade.

William P. Breen remained a tenant through the early part of the decade, while the copycat house next door transitioned to owner-occupancy under William Johnson. The Puhek family was already living at 1147 McKinstry before they officially owned it—a 1925 newspaper account records John Puhek Jr. being removed from the house.[10] On September 1, 1927, the Steadley heirs (Anna Steadley and others) officially sold the property to John and Gertrude Puhek, ending its decades-long stint as a rental and integrating it into a multi-decade family legacy.

The 1930s–1950s: The Puhek Family Era and Mid-Century Detroit

This span encompassed the profound economic shock of the Great Depression, the mobilization of Detroit as the “Arsenal of Democracy” during World War II, and the initial post-war suburban migration waves. Unlike many areas that hollowed out quickly, Southwest Detroit retained strong immigrant and working-class community networks.

In the 1930 census, John Puhek is recorded as the owner-occupant of 1147 McKinstry.[11] Gertrude Puhek passed away in 1934 at age 56, and John Puhek died in 1938. His estate entered probate (file #260825), with heirs including daughters Mary, Gertrude, Agnes, and sons Matthew, Joseph, and Frank. Ownership consolidated to Mary Puhek through quit-claim deeds executed in January 1941. Through the 1950s, Mary lived at the house alongside siblings Mathew, Agnes, and Joseph—all working in the nearby auto plants.[12]

The 1960s–1980s: Continuity Through Urban Shifts

During these decades, Detroit experienced significant demographic shifts, economic restructuring, and the acceleration of regional white flight. However, Southwest Detroit—bolstered by its strong Hispanic roots and the growing cultural identity of Mexicantown—retained its residential stability relative to other parts of the city.

In August 1960, ownership formally transitioned from Mary to Agnes Puhek, who maintained the property. Sibling Mathew J. Puhek (born February 25, 1909) eventually moved to Farmington before passing away in October 1969. Agnes continued as owner through the 1970s and likely into the 1980s, completing the Puhek family's nearly 70-year tenure on McKinstry Street.

The 1990s–Present: Cultural Shift and Modern Restoration

Mexicantown emerged as one of Detroit's most resilient and culturally vibrant neighborhoods, successfully bucking trends of abandonment through active community preservation, local commerce, and new waves of investment.

After nearly seven decades under the Puhek family, Agnes Puhek sold the house in 1996 to Mary J. Medina. Agnes passed away in 1998. In 1999, ownership transferred to Alfredo and Maria D. Garcia, who held the property for a quarter-century, utilizing it as a rental toward the latter half of their tenure. In June 2024, the house was purchased by Kyle Hooks, with the mortgage fully paid off in August 2025—marking a new chapter for the late-19th-century home as Mexicantown and Springwells experience renewed stability and appreciation in 21st-century Detroit.
